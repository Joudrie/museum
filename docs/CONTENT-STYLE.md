# Content style: how we write about art

Every blurb should make someone want to look closer (VISION #6). Short, true, curious.
The pipeline (`docs/CONTENT.md`) enforces the mechanical parts; this is the judgment part.

## The rule behind all the others
**A claim is only as good as the sentence it came from.** Every blurb and fact is built from a
claim, and every claim carries the exact museum sentence it rests on (`quote`) and which field that
was (`source`). If you can't point at a sentence, the claim doesn't go in. Leaving a blurb blank is
fine; inventing one is not.

## Length
- **Blurb:** one or two sentences, at most **220 characters**. It is shown on a phone under the painting.
- **Facts:** one to three extra lines, at most **200 characters** each. They are the rabbit hole.

## Voice
- Plain, warm, specific. Like a good friend at the next painting, not a plaque.
- Lead with the surprising, concrete thing: who, what they did, how long it took, what was hidden,
  what survived. One real detail beats three adjectives.
- Present tense for what you can see, past tense for what happened.
- Numbers are fine, but only if they are in the source. The checker refuses a number that isn't in the quote.
- Name people the way the museum does. Don't guess at motives or feelings.

## What to avoid
- Filler: *masterpiece, iconic, famous, renowned, stunning, breathtaking, beautiful, magnificent, timeless,
  genius, legendary, world-class, extraordinary*. The checker flags these.
- "This painting" / "It" as an opening when the title isn't on screen with it.
- Rankings and superlatives the source doesn't make ("the greatest…"). If the museum says "one of the
  earliest", say that, not "the first".
- Explaining the painting's meaning as settled when scholars argue about it.
- Sneaking in facts from memory. If you know something true that the source lacks, find a source
  and quote it, or leave it out.

## Wording uncertainty
Say what is known and how sure we are, in the museum's own words where possible.
- Dates: keep the museum's form ("ca. 1550", "1780s"). Don't tidy a range into a single year.
- "probably", "may have", "is thought to", "according to the museum" are good. Don't remove them to sound confident.
- When something is unknown: "No one knows who…" is a fact if the museum says so.

## Contested attribution
- If the record says "attributed to", "workshop of", "follower of", "after" or "circle of", say that.
  Never promote it to the artist's name in a blurb.
- If scholars disagree, say so in one line and stop. Don't pick a side.
- The `by` field keeps the museum's maker string. Don't edit a maker to a more famous name.

## Sensitive subjects
Some works show or come from violence, colonisation, slavery, religion, the dead, or people who
did not choose to be painted. Real art includes them, and we can include them honestly.
- **Describe plainly** what is shown and what the work was made for. No jokes, no cute framing.
- **Use people's own names** and the names communities use for themselves, where the record gives
  them. Don't repeat outdated terms except inside a quoted museum title (and then keep the title as
  the museum gives it; mention in `notes` that a reviewer should look at it).
- **Say who made it and for whom** when the record does. A painting of a people by an outsider is an
  outsider's picture; say so if the museum does.
- Casta paintings, colonial portraits, battle scenes, funerary art: state the purpose and stop. Don't decorate.
- Sacred objects: say what the object is for, not that it is "exotic".
- Nudity and religion are ordinary in art history; describe them in the same flat, factual tone.
- When in doubt, leave the draft in review with a note rather than approving it.

## Non-European and non-Western work
The game shouldn't treat Europe as the default. Don't compare a work to European art to explain it.
Use the maker's and culture's own terms (ukiyo-e, hanging scroll, miniature, icon) and explain them in a few words.

## Rarity and era
- **Rarity** (1 common to 4 legendary) is a decision, not a default: how well known the work is to a general
  visitor. Set it at review, or add "rarity ok" to the notes for a deliberate 1.
- **Era** is a date bucket (Renaissance before 1600, Baroque to 1750, 1700s–1850s, Impressionist to 1900, Modern after),
  not a claim about style. A Japanese print from 1832 lands in "1700s–1850s". That's fine.
